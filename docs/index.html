A Multi-Sensory Dataset of Embodied Interaction with Reconstructed Autobiographical Spaces

A large-scale, synchronized corpus capturing how human memory shapes spatial perception inside a fifty-year family home.

Daniel Pillis
Massachusetts Institute of Technology  ·  Emerson College
IEEE / CVF Conference on Computer Vision and Pattern Recognition  —  CVPR 2026
Paper arXiv Poster Code Dataset

Abstract

Embodied intelligence requires models of how humans perceive and navigate space, yet most spatial datasets capture passive, single-modal data rather than active, perception-based interaction. We present the Jefferson Drive Simulation Dataset (JDSD), a large-scale multi-sensory corpus capturing high-resolution 2D and 3D samples of a spatial environment — including tactile, olfactory, and interaction data — designed to capture the sensory experience of a specific space. We chose a memory-laden family home as a vehicle for creating a sensory–object–memory corpus.

Over the course of a year we performed an exhaustive capture of a domestic environment in Pittsburgh, PA, a single-family home lived in by the same residents for over fifty years. By digitally scanning the available photographic data, analog video, three-dimensional maps, 360° photos of each room, and comprehensive data about the space, we reconstructed the environment at high fidelity. To add semantic and sensory content, participants with lived experience of the space were instrumented with XR head-mounted displays, spatial pose trackers, and tangible haptic interfaces while exploring a Gaussian-splat reconstruction spanning ~50 years of occupancy. Synchronized streams of egocentric video, 6-DOF pose, gaze, audio, and tangible object interaction were recorded — creating a unique setting where memory, interaction, and perception are in dialogue with multimodal media.

Totaling approximately 4 TB across 800+ scanned objects and 18 rooms, the dataset provides a novel benchmark for studying multi-modal spatial understanding and embodied interaction with reconstructed environments, with applications in generalizable representations for manipulation in robotics and XR.

02

A house, remembered

The reconstruction focuses on a single-family residential home on Jefferson Drive in Pittsburgh, PA — lived in by the same residents for over five decades and documented from 1965 to 2025 across celluloid film, analog photography, sound recordings, and high-definition video.

Rather than another anonymous indoor scan, the home was chosen precisely because it is saturated with memory. Its eighteen rooms, heirloom objects, and recurring family rituals make it an ideal vehicle for studying how autobiographical experience shapes the way people see, reach, and move through space.

03

High-fidelity reconstruction

A four-part capture pipeline combines structure-from-motion photogrammetry, 3D Gaussian splatting, panoramic photography, and object-level scanning into a hybrid representation that supports both scene-level navigation and object-level interaction.

Gaussian-splat scene reconstruction

Each room was photographed from 60–120 positions at multiple perspectives, then processed with a structure-from-motion pipeline using both geometric photogrammetry and 3D Gaussian splatting to produce photorealistic, real-time-navigable environment representations.

Over 40 hours of 8mm and 16mm home film were digitized, annotated with spatial metadata, and rendered as time-based Gaussian splats transposed back into their original locations within the reconstruction.

04

Sixteen rooms, in 360°

Full equirectangular 360°×180° panoramas of each interior space, captured as single-shot source data for the reconstruction and as a standalone virtual tour.

05

Object-level photogrammetry

More than 800 individual objects from the family's collection — furniture, personal items, ephemera, and decorative pieces — were scanned with a structured-light scanner and cleaned through a customized InstantMeshes retopology workflow into meshes suitable for real-time rendering.

Each scan was manually aligned and added to a library of configurable, room-specific elements, turning the home's contents into an interactive, queryable archive.

06

Five decades of archival media

Thousands of family photographs and over forty hours of home film were digitized and spatially anchored — linking each image and clip to specific rooms and time periods inside the reconstruction.

07

Synchronized multi-sensory capture

During each session, six co-registered streams were recorded simultaneously. The primary device was a Meta Quest 3 providing egocentric RGB-D video and 6-DOF head pose via onboard SLAM, with integrated eye tracking for gaze, an 8-mic array for spatial audio, pressure/proximity sensing on the tangible interfaces, and a directional microphone for transcribed narration.

Participants first described a memory tied to a room, then navigated the reconstruction while recalling it aloud — with original home movies playing on spatially anchored virtual surfaces and physical memory boxes available for tangible interaction in mixed reality.

08

Tangible & olfactory memory boxes

Two book-like wooden containers house original wallpaper samples, carpet fragments, fragrances, and personal artifacts — coupling physical scent and texture with the virtual reconstruction as a cross-modal grounding stimulus.

09

Tentative findings

  • Memory modulates spatial attention. Gaze concentrated on regions tied to verbally recalled content — participants describing Christmas gatherings fixated densely around the dining table and tree; those recalling the basement looked toward stairways and lower-floor doorways.
  • Reaching toward remembered objects. Touch data showed gestures oriented toward virtual object locations from narrated memories, even when objects existed only in the virtual layer with no physical correlate.
  • Cross-modal olfactory–spatial association. Participants who physically handled scented artifacts spent longer dwelling in the virtual rooms associated with those materials than those who did not engage the tangible elements.
  • A natural contrastive structure. The same participants navigating the same space with and without memory cues yields a built-in control/experimental split for learning the differential effect of memory on navigation and attention.

Ethics & consent

The dataset involves deeply personal autobiographical content. Informed consent was obtained from all participants under university IRB, with explicit opt-in per modality. Verbal narratives were reviewed by participants before inclusion, identifying information is pseudonymized, faces in egocentric video are blurred, and the spatial reconstruction will be released only in anonymized form. We advocate for community norms around consent, data minimization, and participant control over spatial personal data.
